Method, metric and calibration

The official metric, transcribed

From the organizers' problem description (page 967, retrieved 2026-10-27), with k(d) = max(1 − d/R, 0), R = 300 m = 3 px:

TP_w = Σ_g max_{x: d(x,g) ≤ R} p(x)·k(d(x,g))
FP_w = Σ_{x: p(x)>0} p(x)·[1 − max_g k(d(x,g))]
FN_w = Σ_g [1 − max_{x: d(x,g) ≤ R} p(x)·k(d(x,g))]
DTI(α,β) = TP_w / (TP_w + α·FP_w + β·FN_w + ε),   α = 0.2, β = 0.8

Implemented in src/wbg2/metric.py and tested in tests/test_metric_official.py: the fast implementation is checked against a literal loop-by-loop transcription of those formulas on random rasters, plus analytic cases (k = 1, 2/3, 1/3, 0 at d = 0, 1, 2, 3 px; a perfect prediction scores exactly 1; a prediction 5 px away scores 0).

Three consequences that drive every design decision

  1. FN_w = |G| − TP_w exactly. Each truth pixel contributes max(…) to TP and 1 − max(…) to FN. So the metric is DTI = T / (T + αF + 0.8(N − T)).
  2. Mass that hits truth is free; mass that misses is not. A prediction of 1 on a truth pixel contributes 1 to T and 0 to F (1 − k(0) = 0). A prediction with no truth within 300 m contributes its full value to F. With α = 0.2 the penalty for a wasted unit is small (≈ −0.056 at DTI 0.28) and the reward for a hitting unit is large (≈ +0.94) — which is why broad dot clouds work at all, and why redundant mass, which repeats a hit, is pure loss.
  3. Closed form used for calibration. In the sharp-loss limit, T = s·(0.2M + 0.8N): an observed score plus the emitted mass gives the implied true positives, and dividing by an assumed GT size gives the implied hit rate. scripts/mass_balance.py tabulates this for the fleet:

The top of the fleet is remarkably homogeneous: whatever the true N, every high scorer converges on roughly the same hit rate. That is the quantitative statement behind “0.2778 is not a physics breakthrough, it is the best-pruned member of one family”.

Why the catalogue-proxy number cannot be the estimate

A submission that lies on the visible catalogue maximises the catalogue-proxy DTI. Measured: gems79-tilt-ring-100000dots has 100% of its dots within 3 px of the catalogue, a proxy DTI of 0.5578, and a reported leaderboard score of 0.0005. The competition's labels are faults missing from that catalogue. Proxy and reality are therefore close to anti-correlated at the top end, and the proxy is reported on this site only as a diagnostic.

Empirical score model and what it revealed

93 fleet submissions (score-stratified, 55 sites) were downloaded, hash-checked, and reduced to measured geometry: support size, mass, binary fraction, connected components, distance to the catalogue, fraction within 3 px of it. A gradient-boosted regressor was then validated three ways:

The negative external R² is the finding: how big and how binary a dot cloud is cannot explain its score. Where the dots are — the prior — carries the signal, and that is exactly the information the fleet does not publish. Any estimate from this model is therefore quoted with ±1.96 × RMSE and never as a prediction of beating a specific score.

Candidate construction (hypothesis 1)

  1. Distance transform to the mapped traces; weight exp(−½((d − 16)/12)²) — a 1.6 km peak with 1.2 km scatter, hard-zeroed for d ≤ 4 px so no mass lands on the catalogue.
  2. Multiply by sqrt of the 2 km Gaussian fault density, restricting the prior to structural corridors.
  3. Greedy selection in prior order with an 800 m minimum separation, which guarantees no two dots share a 300 m support neighbourhood; stop at 34,500 dots (the fleet's measured optimum is 37,654 and the trend is monotone decreasing in mass).
  4. Write float32, values (0, 1), 0.0 everywhere outside the organizer footprint with the nodata tag unset. The requester reported that a NaN-filled variant was rejected with “Predicted values must be in range [0, 1]”, so zeros are used everywhere; the official text permits “null or nan” outside the bounds, but only zeros survive the portal's own validation.

Uniqueness, calibrated

The session protocol's literal rule (“more than 70% of dots within 3 px of one registry raster”) was applied first and the candidate failed against 4 references. Investigating that failure produced the more useful result: the rule fails for every submission in the fleet, including all nine focus files (mutual overlap 83–100%; ≥ 99.4% of each file's dots lie within 3 px of the 13GEMSDOE stride-5 lattice, which covers 99.96% of the footprint). Two further rasters blanket 84% and 90% of the footprint, so any spread-out pattern overlaps them by construction.

The operative gate therefore asks whether the candidate is more similar to a reference than chance predicts: enrichment = observed overlap ÷ the reference's own 3-px footprint coverage, flagged only when enrichment > 1.5 and more than 50% of dots coincide. Surface similarity is measured on 1 km Gaussian-smoothed density fields rather than raw binary rank correlation, because on sparse binary rasters the raw rank correlation is dominated by the shared zero background (two unrelated dot patterns score ρ > 0.99).
