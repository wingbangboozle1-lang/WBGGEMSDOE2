Why did the GEMSDOE32 “0.2778” result win, and can we beat it?

A PhD-level, byte-verified reading — not a score projection.

Evidence review

We reopened the exact historical bytes. The GEMSDOE32 B2 raster is bit-for-bit the earlier D2.8 dotted line (GEMSDOE25 = GEMSDOE30, 44,090 dots) with every dot within 2 px of the mapped-fault catalogue removed — 6,436 dots pruned, leaving 37,654. GEMSDOE36 then re-added 1,200 dots and its claim fell slightly (0.2750). This is measured lineage, not proof the bytes were submitted or caused the score.

Mechanism (DTI, α=0.2, β=0.8, 300 m triangular kernel): the metric rewards probability mass placed within 300 m of a true (hidden) fault pixel, while false negatives are penalised 4× false positives. A sparse, evenly spaced dotted line along structural ridges keeps kernel coverage of many possible true traces while limiting redundant false-positive mass. Pruning the 2 px halo around mapped faults removes dots that the organizer masks from scoring anyway, so they cannot earn credit — raising the ratio without losing coverage. Sparse → less FP mass; catalogue-pruned → no wasted dots on known traces. That is a plausible explanation for the ordering 0.2778 > 0.2750 > 0.2600.

Can we beat it? Not provably from here. 0.2778, 0.3195 and 0.4413 are user-reported observations of different leaderboards/prize rounds, not receipts tied to these bytes, and the private truth is hidden — so any “estimated winning score” would be a hallucination. What we can do is submit a genuinely different, independently-motivated prediction (H1) so it is not merely re-testing the same d2.8 ridge family; the organizer’s returned score — and only that — tells us whether it is better.
